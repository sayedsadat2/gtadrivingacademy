This final classroom session brings the course together: what to do when something goes wrong, how to plan responsibly before you even start the car, and a review that connects every module back to the ten hours of in-vehicle instruction that follow.

Session content

Vehicle emergencies

A tire blowout calls for a firm grip, easing off the accelerator (not braking hard), and steering straight while the vehicle slows on its own before pulling over. Brake failure calls for pumping the brake pedal, downshifting if possible, and using the parking brake gradually — never yank it at speed. Steering that suddenly feels heavy or unresponsive is often a power-steering failure — the vehicle is still steerable, just heavier; stay calm and keep both hands on the wheel.

If you're involved in a collision, there's a specific order that matters: ensure your own safety first, pull to the side of the road if you can, stop immediately, warn other traffic if it's safe to do so, call for medical help if anyone is injured, and call the police. From there: exchange name, address, licence number, plate number and insurance information with the other driver regardless of fault, and collect names and addresses of any witnesses — they may not still be there if you wait. Stay at the scene, complete any required accident report, and where one is available, attend a collision reporting centre — common across the GTA for minor collisions without injury. If you've been injured, see a doctor even if it doesn't feel urgent at the time; some injuries aren't obvious until later.

Ontario requires reporting a collision to police when there's injury, or when damage appears to exceed the current reporting threshold — confirm the current dollar threshold, since it's adjusted periodically. If you're stopped by a police officer for any reason, pull safely to the side of the road and have your licence and registration ready.

Being passed by an emergency vehicle: pull toward the right edge of the road and stop until it has passed, unless directed otherwise by a police officer.

Trip planning, done responsibly

Real trip planning covers more than the route: weather along the way, fuel or charge level, how much time you actually have versus how much the trip needs, who's in the vehicle, what's in it, and whether you personally are fit to make the drive. An emergency kit — basic tools, a first aid kit, seasonal supplies — is part of planning, not an afterthought.

Environmentally responsible driving is also part of planning: maintaining steady speeds and avoiding unnecessary idling improves fuel efficiency, keeping up with mandatory emissions testing keeps your vehicle compliant, and disposing of fluids, batteries and tires properly (rather than in household trash) matters for the same reason wearing a seatbelt does — it's a routine responsibility, not an inconvenience.

Driving as a lifelong learning process

Nobody finishes learning to drive when they pass a road test. Vehicle technology changes (advanced driver-assist features, electric vehicles, changing dashboard interfaces), laws and regulations change, and your own skills and judgment keep developing well past your first year on the road. Treating driving as something you continue learning — rather than a box checked once — is itself one of the strongest predictors of a long, collision-free driving record.

This also applies in the other direction: driving ability and judgment can decline with age, illness, or medication changes, and recognizing that in yourself — or supporting a family member through that recognition — is part of being a responsible, safety-minded driver over a lifetime, not just in your first year.

Bringing it together

Every module in this course feeds into the same ten hours of in-vehicle instruction ahead of you: the rules from Module 1 are what your instructor expects you to already know at an intersection; the hazard-scanning habits from Module 7 are what they'll be watching for on every drive; the emotional self-management from Module 4 is what keeps a stressful moment from becoming a bad decision.

None of this replaces supervised practice — it's what makes that practice worth more, because you'll already understand why your instructor is asking you to do something, not just following instructions.

Key terminology

Reporting threshold
The damage amount above which a collision must be reported to police in Ontario; adjusted periodically.
Emergency kit
Basic tools, first aid supplies and seasonal items kept in the vehicle for roadside emergencies.
Environmentally responsible driving
Practices such as reducing idling, proper fluid/tire disposal, and maintaining emissions compliance.
Lifelong learning (driving)
The ongoing development of driving skill and knowledge well beyond initial licensing.
Power-steering failure
A malfunction that makes steering heavier but does not eliminate the ability to steer.
Course integration
Connecting the classroom modules to what is practiced during in-vehicle instruction.
Collision reporting centre
A facility, common across the GTA, where minor collisions without injury can be formally reported.
