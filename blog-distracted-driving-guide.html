The Hidden Dangers of Distracted Driving — A Parent's Guide

Jul 7, 2024
Wais Bashari
6 min read
4,412 views

Distracted driving has become one of the leading causes of collisions on Ontario roads, and new drivers are disproportionately affected. As instructors, we see it firsthand — a student doing everything right until a notification buzzes, a friend asks a question, or the radio needs adjusting. Here's what every parent should understand.

It's More Than Just Phones

When people hear "distracted driving," they think texting. But distraction falls into three categories: visual (eyes off the road), manual (hands off the wheel), and cognitive (mind off driving) — and phones are actually the worst offenders because they combine all three at once. But eating, adjusting a GPS, reaching for something in the back seat, or even an intense conversation all count too.

Why New Drivers Are Especially at Risk

Experienced drivers have built enough automatic skill that basic driving tasks require less conscious effort, freeing up some mental bandwidth. New drivers don't have that yet — every lane change, every intersection, every merge still requires active concentration. That means a distraction that an experienced driver might absorb without incident can be genuinely dangerous for someone still building those instincts.

"A new driver's brain is working harder just to drive normally. There's less spare capacity left over to handle a distraction safely." — Wais Bashari, Instructor & Founder

Ontario's Distracted Driving Laws

Ontario has some of the strictest distracted driving penalties in Canada. Handheld device use while driving carries fines starting at several hundred dollars, demerit points, and for novice (G1/G2) drivers specifically, a licence suspension is possible even on a first offence.

What to do: Make phone use a firm, non-negotiable rule for new drivers — not "only at red lights," which itself is illegal and still risky, but genuinely off and out of reach while driving.

Passengers as a Distraction

This is the one parents most often overlook. Ontario's graduated licensing system limits G2 drivers to specific passenger restrictions in the first months for exactly this reason — research consistently shows collision risk rises sharply with each additional teen passenger in the car.

What to do: Know and respect the G2 passenger restrictions during the required period. Even once they no longer legally apply, easing into full passenger freedom gradually is safer than an abrupt jump.

How to Talk to Your Teen About It

Lecturing tends to backfire. Teens who feel talked down to often tune out the message entirely. What works better is treating it as a shared problem-solving conversation rather than a rule handed down from above.

What to do: Ask your teen directly what situations make them want to check their phone while driving, and problem-solve those specific triggers together — for example, turning on "Do Not Disturb While Driving" before starting the car, every time, as an automatic habit rather than a willpower test.
