Licensing, Rules of the Road and Responsible Choices

What you'll be able to do

  • Connect legal compliance to responsible, courteous decision-making — not just “what's legal.”

This session is the foundation for everything else in the course. Before you touch a vehicle, you need to understand the legal framework you're driving inside — Ontario's graduated licensing system, the rules that govern right-of-way, and the consequences that follow when those rules aren't followed.

Session content

Ontario's graduated licensing system

Ontario uses a three-step graduated licensing system: G1, G2, and full G. Each step exists because collision risk for new drivers drops sharply with supervised experience — the system is built around that fact, not around making things difficult.

As a G1 driver, you must be accompanied by a fully licensed driver (four or more years, under 0.05 BAC) in the front passenger seat, you cannot drive between midnight and 5 a.m., you cannot drive on 400-series highways or high-speed expressways unless a certified driving instructor is with you, and your blood alcohol concentration must be zero. G2 removes the accompanying-driver and highway restrictions but keeps the zero blood-alcohol condition and adds passenger limits during late-night hours for drivers under 20.

Completing an MTO-recognized Beginner Driver Education course like this one is what allows you to take your G2 road test after 8 months instead of 12, and G road test after 12 months from G2 instead of 24 — and it's typically what qualifies you for an insurance discount. Confirm current wait-time rules with the ministry when you book your test, since these are set by regulation and can change.

Why it matters: almost every G1/G2 suspension we see traces back to one of three things — driving after midnight, driving unaccompanied, or a non-zero BAC. These aren't obscure rules; they're the ones most likely to end your licence early.

Signs, signals and pavement markings

Ontario groups signs by shape and colour, and the shape alone should tell you what kind of message you're getting before you can even read the text. Octagons are always stop. Triangles pointing down are always yield. Diamonds are warning signs — something ahead requires caution. Rectangles are regulatory (speed limits, parking rules) or informational, depending on colour.

Pavement markings carry just as much legal weight as signs. A solid yellow line means no passing from your side; a broken yellow line means passing is permitted when safe. White lines separate traffic moving in the same direction. Where a marking and a sign seem to disagree, the more restrictive rule generally applies — and when in doubt, the safer interpretation wins.

Right-of-way is given, not taken

This is the single most misunderstood phrase in the Highway Traffic Act. No one has the right-of-way by default — the law describes who must yield to whom. You don't “have” the right-of-way; the other driver yields it to you, or doesn't. That distinction matters because a driver who insists on their “right-of-way” into a collision is still partly at fault, and still just as injured.

At an all-way stop, the first vehicle to arrive and come to a full stop goes first. If two vehicles arrive together, the vehicle on the right goes first. At an uncontrolled intersection, the same right-hand rule applies. None of this overrides pedestrians already in a crosswalk, or emergency vehicles responding to a call — those situations require yielding regardless of arrival order.

School buses, railway crossings and emergency vehicles

When a school bus's upper alternating red lights are flashing and its stop arm is extended, traffic in both directions on most roads must stop — the exception is a road with a median or physical barrier separating the directions of travel. This is one of the few rules in the HTA carrying an automatic fine and demerit points on first conviction, with a possible licence suspension on a repeat.

At a railway crossing with flashing lights or a lowered gate, stop and do not proceed until the lights stop flashing and the gate fully rises — never drive around a lowered gate. For an emergency vehicle with lights and siren active, pull toward the right edge of the road and stop; Ontario's move-over law also requires slowing and, where safe, changing lanes away from a stopped emergency vehicle, tow truck, or utility vehicle with its lights active.

Key terminology

Graduated licensing
Ontario's three-stage licensing system — G1, G2, full G — that adds driving privileges as supervised experience increases.
Right-of-way
A privilege that must be yielded by another driver; it cannot be legally “taken” by insisting on it.
Controlled intersection
An intersection governed by a traffic signal or sign (stop/yield) rather than right-of-way rules alone.
Move-over law
The requirement to slow down and, where safe, change lanes away from a stopped emergency, tow, or utility vehicle with lights active.
Demerit points
Points recorded against a licence for specific offences; accumulating enough can trigger a suspension or interview.
Novice driver
A G1 or G2 licence holder, subject to conditions such as the zero blood-alcohol requirement.
