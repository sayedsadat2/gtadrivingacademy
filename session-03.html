Vehicle Handling: Space, Turns and Low-Speed Manoeuvres

120 minutes Digital classroom

What you'll be able to do

  • Select safe speed and lane position for current conditions.
  • Apply the observation-signal-position-speed-look sequence to turns.
  • Plan safe reversing, parking and turnaround manoeuvres, and recognize when evasive action is needed.

This session moves from knowing the vehicle to actually placing it — in a lane, through a turn, into a parking space. The physical skills matter, but so does the decision-making underneath them: why you chose that lane position, that following distance, that moment to turn.

Session content

Space and following distance

Following distance is measured in time, not metres, because time scales automatically with speed. The standard method: pick a fixed point on the road, and count the seconds from when the vehicle ahead passes it to when you reach it. A minimum safe gap in good conditions still needs to grow for rain, snow, darkness, heavy traffic, towing, or fatigue — any one of those on its own is a reason to open the gap further.

Lane position isn't just about staying inside the lines. Positioning slightly toward one side of your lane can increase your visibility around a vehicle ahead, keep you further from a hazard on one side, or make you more visible to a driver waiting to turn out of a side street.

Turns: the OSPSL sequence

Every turn follows the same structure: Observe (check mirrors and blind spot), Signal (early, so others can react), Position (move into the correct lane position for the turn), Speed (adjust before you're in the turn, not during it), and Look (a final check of the path you're turning into, including pedestrians and cyclists who may not be where you expect).

The most common mistake isn't forgetting to signal — it's braking mid-turn because speed wasn't adjusted beforehand. Slow before the turn, on a straight line, so the vehicle is stable and predictable through the turn itself.

Watch for: a right turn on red requires a full stop first, then checking for pedestrians and cyclists who have the right to continue straight through the crosswalk.

Reversing, parking, and evasive manoeuvres

Reversing is the manoeuvre most likely to involve a collision with something you didn't see, because your natural field of view is smaller looking backward. Turn and look through the rear window for the actual manoeuvre; a backup camera or mirrors alone aren't a substitute for a physical head turn.

Parallel, perpendicular and angle parking each use reference points — consistent spots on your own vehicle that line up with the space you're aiming for. The exact reference points vary by vehicle, which is why the first few attempts in a new vehicle should be slow and deliberate. Hill parking adds another step: angle your wheels toward the curb facing downhill, or away from the curb facing uphill with no curb, so a rolling vehicle is stopped by the curb rather than rolling into traffic.

Sometimes there isn't time to brake to a stop — a child steps out, an object appears in the lane. Basic evasive manoeuvres (steering around versus braking, or both together within traction limits) are a last resort, not a first plan. Specific situations call for specific responses: for a potential head-on collision, steer right toward the shoulder rather than crossing further into oncoming lanes; for a rear-end risk, cover the brake early and increase following distance rather than waiting for the moment you need to stop hard; if your wheels drop off the paved surface, ease off the accelerator, grip firmly, and steer straight back onto the pavement gradually — a sharp, sudden correction is what causes a rollover, not the initial drop itself. In any of these, threshold braking — braking as hard as possible just short of lock-up — gives you the shortest stopping distance while keeping some steering control.

Traffic circles and roundabouts

Roundabouts are becoming more common across the GTA, and they work differently from a normal intersection: traffic already circulating has the right-of-way, and vehicles entering must yield to it, not the other way around. Slow down on approach, look left for a safe gap (traffic circulates counter-clockwise in Ontario), and enter without stopping if the way is clear.

Choose your lane based on your exit before you enter — stay in the outer lane for the first exit, use an inner lane if you're going further around, and signal right just before your exit, not while still circulating. Never stop inside a roundabout except to avoid a collision.

Knowledge check

Key terminology

Following distance
The time gap kept between your vehicle and the one ahead, measured in seconds using a fixed reference point.
OSPSL
Observe, Signal, Position, Speed, Look — the sequence used for safe turns.
Reference point
A consistent spot on your own vehicle used to judge position during parking or reversing manoeuvres.
Evasive manoeuvre
A last-resort steering and/or braking response to an unavoidable, sudden hazard.
Lane position
Where you place the vehicle within its lane, adjusted for visibility, hazards and other road users.
Tire inflation
Correct tire pressure, which directly affects handling, traction and stopping distance.
Roundabout
A circular intersection where entering traffic yields to vehicles already circulating.
Threshold braking
Braking as hard as possible just short of wheel lock-up, for the shortest stopping distance with steering control retained.
