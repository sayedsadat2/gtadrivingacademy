CC7 · Attention

Attention, Distraction, Impairment and Fitness to Drive

120 minutes Digital classroom

What you'll be able to do

  • Distinguish visual, manual, cognitive and auditory distraction, and recognize combined distraction.
  • Explain impairment from alcohol, drugs, fatigue, illness and medication accurately, without relying on myths.
  • Choose lawful, safe alternatives whenever you're not fit to drive.

Attention is the resource every other skill in this course depends on — hazard perception, reaction time, decision-making, all of it requires attention that's actually available, not divided or impaired. This session covers what pulls attention away, and what removes it entirely.

Session content

Four types of distraction

Visual distraction takes your eyes off the road — looking at a phone, a passenger, or an object on the seat. Manual distraction takes your hands off the wheel. Cognitive distraction takes your mind off driving even when eyes and hands are technically in place — daydreaming, an intense conversation, working through a problem. Auditory distraction is anything competing for your attention through sound.

Texting is the clearest example of combined distraction, because it involves all four types at once: eyes down, hand off the wheel, mind on the message, and often a notification sound pulling focus again. That combination is why texting-related collision risk is measured in multiples, not percentages, compared to undistracted driving.

Impairment: separating fact from myth

Alcohol impairs judgment and reaction time well before a driver feels “drunk” — measurable impairment begins with the first drink, not at the legal limit. Common myths (coffee sobers you up, a short nap fixes it, you can tell when you're okay to drive) are all false; the only thing that reduces blood alcohol concentration is time.

Cannabis and other drugs — including many prescription and over-the-counter medications — can impair reaction time, attention, and judgment in ways that don't always feel obvious to the person affected. Ontario's zero blood-alcohol requirement for G1/G2 drivers applies regardless of what substance is involved; “I feel fine” is not a legal or reliable standard.

Fatigue impairs driving in ways that closely resemble alcohol impairment — slowed reaction time, poor judgment, and in severe cases, microsleep, where a driver's eyes are open but they're briefly unconscious. There is no way to “push through” fatigue safely; the only real fix is rest.

Zero BAC condition: as a novice driver, your blood alcohol concentration must be zero — this is stricter than the 0.05/0.08 limits that apply to fully licensed drivers, and it applies for as long as you hold a G1 or G2 licence.

The four components of attention

“Pay attention” isn't one skill — it's actually four distinct abilities working together, and knowing them individually helps you notice which one is failing in a given moment. Selective attention is filtering relevant information from irrelevant — picking the pedestrian out of a busy streetscape. Sustained attention (vigilance) is maintaining consistent focus over time, which is exactly what erodes on a long, monotonous drive. Divided attention (shifting) is moving focus between tasks — checking a mirror, then returning to the road ahead. Mental tracking is holding several pieces of information in mind at once, like remembering your exit is coming up while also watching the vehicle ahead.

Dividing attention between driving and any secondary task — even a legal, low-effort one — reduces the mental bandwidth available for hazard detection. The safest approach isn't managing distraction well; it's eliminating it before the drive starts — phone out of reach, destination set before moving, passengers briefed if needed.

If you're not fit to drive — impaired, exhausted, seriously distracted by a personal situation — the alternatives are straightforward: call someone, use a taxi or rideshare, or simply wait. None of those options are as costly as a collision, a suspension, or worse.

Key terminology

Visual distraction
Anything that takes your eyes off the road.
Manual distraction
Anything that takes your hands off the controls.
Cognitive distraction
Anything that takes your mind off driving, regardless of where your eyes or hands are.
Zero BAC condition
The requirement that G1/G2 drivers have a blood alcohol concentration of zero while driving.
Microsleep
A brief, involuntary period of unconsciousness with eyes open, caused by severe fatigue.
Fitness to drive
A driver's actual physical and mental capacity to drive safely at a given moment.
Selective attention
The ability to filter relevant information from irrelevant while driving.
Sustained attention (vigilance)
The ability to maintain consistent focus over time, which erodes on long or monotonous drives.
