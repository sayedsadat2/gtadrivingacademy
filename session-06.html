CC6 · Sharing the Road

Sharing the Road and Respectful Mobility

120 minutes Digital classroom

What you'll be able to do

  • Identify the needs and vulnerabilities of pedestrians, cyclists, motorcyclists and transit vehicles.
  • Apply safe passing, following and yielding practices specific to each type of road user.
  • Demonstrate cooperative, patient driving in mixed traffic.

The GTA's roads carry more than cars — pedestrians, cyclists, transit, motorcycles and delivery vehicles all share the same space you drive in. This session is about the specific risks each group faces, and the specific adjustments a driver makes to protect them.

Session content

Pedestrians and cyclists: the most vulnerable road users

Pedestrians and cyclists have no protective shell — the margin for driver error around them is much smaller than around another vehicle. At a crosswalk, a pedestrian who has stepped off the curb has the right to complete their crossing safely, and a driver waiting to turn must yield until they've cleared the relevant lane.

Ontario law requires drivers to leave at least one metre when passing a cyclist, more where speeds are higher. Cyclists are entitled to a full lane where the lane is too narrow to share safely — a cyclist riding in the centre of a narrow lane isn't being difficult, they're avoiding the door zone and making themselves visible.

Motorcycles and transit

Motorcycles are smaller and easier to misjudge in distance and speed than a car — a common cause of collisions is a driver turning left across a motorcycle's path because they misjudged how close or how fast it was approaching. Always do a dedicated check for motorcycles before a turn, not just a glance for “any traffic.”

A transit bus signalling to pull out from a stop has the right of way to re-enter traffic in most Ontario municipalities — slow down and let it in rather than trying to beat it to the gap. Streetcars in Toronto add another layer: passengers may board or exit directly into the traffic lane at some stops, and traffic must stop behind an open streetcar door unless a safety island separates you.

School zones and playground zones carry reduced speed limits during posted hours specifically because children are less predictable and less visible than adult pedestrians — treat the posted times as a hard rule, not a suggestion.

Traffic control persons

At construction sites, collisions, or special events, a traffic control person (often called a flag person) may direct traffic by hand signal or sign. Their direction overrides the normal rules of the road — including signs and signals — for as long as they're actively directing traffic. If a flag person's signal seems to conflict with a traffic light, follow the flag person.

Approach a traffic control zone at reduced speed with extra following distance, since the person directing traffic is themselves a vulnerable road user standing in or near live traffic.

Cooperative driving

Cooperative driving means making your intentions predictable — signalling early, maintaining consistent speed, and giving other road users room to make their own decisions safely. It also means not treating every gap in traffic as something to compete for.

In mixed, unpredictable traffic — construction zones, weather, a busy commercial street — patience isn't passive. It's an active choice to accept a slightly longer trip in exchange for a meaningfully lower chance of a collision involving someone with far less protection than you have.

Key terminology

Door zone
The area beside a parked car where an opening door could strike a passing cyclist.
Safety island
A raised platform separating a streetcar stop from the adjacent traffic lane.
Cooperative driving
Predictable, patient driving that gives other road users room to act safely.
Vulnerable road user
A road user with little or no protective structure, such as a pedestrian, cyclist, or motorcyclist.
School zone
A posted area with reduced speed limits during specific hours to protect child pedestrians.
Right-of-way (transit)
The priority given to a transit vehicle signalling to re-enter traffic from a stop.
Traffic control person
A person (e.g., a construction flag person) directing traffic by hand signal, whose direction overrides signs and signals.
