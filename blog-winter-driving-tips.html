Winter Driving in Ontario: 7 Tips to Keep Your Family Safe

Nov 28, 2024
Fahim Yarzai
7 min read
6,104 views

If you've lived in the GTA through even one winter, you know the drill: one day it's clear pavement, the next it's black ice hiding under a dusting of fresh snow. Every year we see the same pattern with new drivers — overconfidence in the first snowfall, followed by a hard lesson about how different winter driving really is. Here are the seven things we drill into every student before their first icy lesson.

1. Prep Your Vehicle Before the First Snow

Winter driving skill starts before you even turn the key. Proper winter tires (not just all-seasons) make a measurable difference in stopping distance on cold pavement — winter tires stay flexible below 7°C, while all-seasons harden and lose grip. Check your tire tread, top up windshield washer fluid with a winter-rated formula, and make sure your battery is tested — cold weather is when weak batteries fail.

What to do: Book a pre-winter check with your mechanic in October or November, before the rush. Don't wait for the first snowfall to discover your tires are worn down.

"Most winter accidents I see aren't caused by bad drivers — they're caused by good drivers who didn't adjust their habits for the conditions." — Fahim Yarzai, Instructor

2. Slow Down More Than You Think You Need To

New drivers consistently underestimate how much speed needs to drop in snow and ice. A speed that feels perfectly normal on dry pavement can leave you with zero margin for error on a snow-covered road. The posted speed limit assumes dry, clear conditions — it is not a target in winter weather.

What to do: As a rule of thumb, reduce your speed by at least a third in snow, and by half or more on icy roads. If other cars are passing you, that's fine — better to arrive late than not at all.

3. Triple Your Following Distance

The standard "3-second rule" for following distance simply doesn't apply in winter. Stopping distances on snow and ice can be two to ten times longer than on dry pavement, depending on conditions. We teach students to stretch their following distance to at least 8–10 seconds in snowy conditions.

What to do: Pick a fixed point ahead — a sign, a pole — and count how long it takes you to reach it after the car ahead passes it. If it's under 8 seconds in winter conditions, back off.

4. Recognizing and Reacting to Black Ice

Black ice is the single most dangerous winter driving hazard because it's nearly invisible — it forms as a thin, transparent layer that lets the black pavement show through. It's most common on bridges, overpasses, and shaded areas, which cool faster than open road.

What to do: Treat bridges and shaded roads with extra caution any time the temperature is near freezing, even if the rest of the road looks dry. If you do hit a patch of black ice, ease off the gas — don't brake — and steer straight until you're through it.

5. What to Do If You Start to Skid

Skids panic new drivers into overcorrecting, which usually makes things worse. The right response depends on the type of skid, but the universal first step is the same: stay calm, ease off the accelerator, and look — and steer — toward where you want the car to go, not at what you're afraid of hitting.

What to do: Practice this response in a controlled setting before you need it in a real emergency — our seasonal Winter Driving Clinic runs students through real skid-recovery drills in a safe, closed environment.

6. Keep Your Visibility Clear — Fully

Clearing a small peephole in your windshield and driving off is one of the most common winter mistakes we see — and it's also a ticketable offence in Ontario. Your mirrors, all windows, headlights, and taillights need to be fully clear so you can see and be seen.

What to do: Budget an extra 5–10 minutes before winter drives specifically for a full clear-off. Keep a proper snow brush and ice scraper in the car at all times, not just when you remember.

7. Keep a Winter Emergency Kit in the Car

Getting stuck or stranded happens even to careful drivers in a bad Ontario storm. A basic kit can turn a stressful situation into a minor inconvenience.

  • Blanket and warm gloves
  • Small shovel and traction aid (sand or kitty litter)
  • Phone charger or power bank
  • Flashlight and extra batteries
  • Snacks and water
