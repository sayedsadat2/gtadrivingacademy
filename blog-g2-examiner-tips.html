What Examiners Actually Look for During Your G2 Road Test

Apr 10, 2024
Wais Bashari
7 min read
9,102 views

A lot of test-day anxiety comes from not knowing what's actually being evaluated. Students imagine examiners are looking for a flawless, textbook-perfect drive — but that's not quite how G2 scoring actually works. Here's what's really happening in that passenger seat.

It's a Checklist, Not a Vibe Check

Examiners are working through a standardized scoring sheet, marking specific observable actions — not forming a general impression of whether they "like" your driving. This is actually good news: it means the test rewards consistent habits over natural-feeling confidence.

Observation Checks Matter More Than You Think

Shoulder checks and mirror checks are scored as discrete, visible actions. An experienced examiner is specifically watching your head movement before lane changes, turns, and merges — not just trusting that you probably checked.

What to do: Make your shoulder checks visibly exaggerated during the test, more than feels natural. An examiner needs to clearly see the head turn, not just sense that you glanced.

"I tell every student the same thing before their test: an examiner can't score what they can't see. Make every check obvious." — Wais Bashari, Instructor & Founder

Smooth Vehicle Control Over Perfection

Jerky braking, harsh acceleration, and rough steering inputs are scored more heavily than most students expect — sometimes more than a single missed maneuver. Smoothness signals genuine control, which is what the test is fundamentally trying to measure.

What to do: In the weeks before your test, focus lessons on smoothness specifically — gentle braking, gradual acceleration — rather than only drilling maneuvers.

Decision-Making at Intersections

Hesitation at intersections — sitting too long at a clear four-way stop, missing a safe gap to turn left — is scored differently than an error, but it's still noted. Examiners are watching for reasonable, timely decisions, not just correctness.

What to do: Practice narrating your decisions out loud during lessons ("that gap is safe, I'm going") to build the habit of committing to a decision rather than freezing.

The Maneuvers: Parking and Three-Point Turns

Parallel parking and three-point (or K-turns) are scored on specific criteria: staying within a set distance of the curb, staying within the road width, and completing the maneuver within a reasonable number of corrections — not requiring a flawless single attempt.

What to do: Know that a small correction mid-maneuver is normal and won't automatically fail you. Panicking and abandoning the attempt is far more costly than a calm adjustment.

The Most Common Reasons for a Fail

  • Rolling through a stop sign without a complete stop
  • Missing a shoulder check before a lane change (even if the lane change itself was safe)
  • Speed inconsistent with posted limits or conditions — too fast or unnecessarily too slow
  • Failing to yield right-of-way at an intersection or to pedestrians
  • Excessive hesitation causing a safety concern for surrounding traffic

None of these are about "talent" — they're specific, practicable habits. That's exactly why we structure lessons around building them deliberately rather than hoping they develop naturally.
