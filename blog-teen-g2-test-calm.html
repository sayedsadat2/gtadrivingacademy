How to Help Your Teen Stay Calm During Their G2 Road Test

Aug 19, 2024
Fahim Yarzai
5 min read
3,977 views

We've watched hundreds of teens walk into a DriveTest centre for their G2 — some calm and focused, others visibly shaking. The difference usually isn't skill. It's how the days leading up to the test were handled, especially at home. Here's what actually helps.

Test-Day Nerves Are Normal — and Manageable

A certain amount of nervous energy is completely normal and even useful — it sharpens focus. The goal isn't to eliminate nerves entirely, it's to keep them from tipping into the kind of panic that causes a capable driver to freeze or make uncharacteristic mistakes.

In the Days Before the Test

The final week before a G2 test isn't the time to cram new skills — it's the time to build confidence in what's already there. Repetition of maneuvers your teen already does well builds more test-day confidence than trying to perfect a weak spot at the last minute.

What to do: Book a confidence-building lesson in the final week that focuses on maneuvers your teen already does well, not new challenges. Familiarity breeds calm.

"The teens who do best on test day aren't always the most skilled drivers in my car — they're the ones who walked in believing they were ready." — Fahim Yarzai, Instructor

The Morning Of

Rushing is the enemy of calm. A stressful morning — running late, forgetting documents, last-minute panic — puts a teen behind the wheel already in a heightened state before the test even begins.

What to do: Lay out all required documents (G1, ID, booking confirmation) the night before. Arrive at least 20 minutes early so there's zero time pressure. Keep the car ride there calm and light — this isn't the moment for last-minute quizzing.

What Not to Say Before the Test

Well-meaning parents sometimes add pressure without realizing it. Phrases like "just don't mess up the parallel park" or "remember, we're paying for this test" put focus on failure rather than performance, and teens pick up on that pressure immediately.

What to do: Stick to calm, simple reassurance: "You've practiced this. Just drive the way you know how." Avoid rehearsing worst-case scenarios right before they go in.

After the Test, Win or Lose

How a parent reacts to a failed test matters more than most people expect. A failed G2 test is common and rarely reflects a teen's actual ability — nerves, an unfamiliar test route, or one unlucky moment can all play a role.

What to do: If they pass, celebrate it fully — this is a genuine achievement. If they don't, keep the response calm and forward-looking: identify what specifically to work on, book a follow-up lesson, and rebook when they're ready. Avoid framing it as a bigger failure than it is.
